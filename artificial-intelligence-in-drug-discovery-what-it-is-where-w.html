Before the paper

A ligand binds a target; a drug must also survive a body and help a patient

It's the difference between a prototype that works on the bench and a device that passes clinical trials, manufacturing, and reimbursement.

The paper insists on a distinction that press releases blur. A ligand binds to a protein, and its endpoints are biochemical: IC50, Ki, Kd. A drug must bind its target and also show acceptable efficacy and safety across preclinical and clinical endpoints. The gap is not cosmetic. A potent ligand can be toxic in vivo and therefore useless; a classic trap is affinity driven by lipophilicity, which wrecks solubility, bioavailability and metabolism. The numbers make the odds concrete: databases like ChEMBL and PubChem hold on the order of 10^6 bioactive ligands, while roughly 10^3 drugs are on the market.

Before the paper

Biological data are conditional: the same experiment gives a different answer when a hidden variable changes

Like a wearable sensor whose reading depends on skin tone, placement and ambient temperature — the number is real, but only under conditions nobody recorded.

The paper argues that biological data differ in kind from images or speech. Results depend on dozens to thousands of variables, and changing one can flip the outcome: imatinib works in Philadelphia chromosome-positive ALL but not in leukaemias driven by other aberrations, and CYP expression can make a fixed dose ineffective. On top of conditionality sits epistemic opacity: we generally lack a solid theoretical account connecting input to output in an experiment. The consequence for machine learning is direct. Labels are conditional, so we often cannot assign meaningful unconditional categories to compound effects — which is exactly what supervised models require.

Before the paper

Selection, deselection and quantification need different performance metrics

A screening test and a confirmatory test in the clinic are judged on different numbers — you tune one for catching everything, the other for being right.

Machine learning models support three different kinds of decisions. In selection settings you prioritise a few candidates out of many, so precision — the hit rate among the ones you test — is what matters. In deselection you screen out bad compounds, so recall among the undesired class matters. In quantification you predict a value for one fixed molecule, so a point-error measure like RMSE is appropriate. The paper's point: model performance must be evaluated in the context of the eventual use case. A generic metric optimised in isolation will not tell you whether the model helps in a real project.

The world before

AI capability in the life sciences has advanced fast, but clinical evidence has not followed

A startup with dazzling demos and a full patent portfolio, but no customers who renewed.

The authors grant that technological capability is genuinely encouraging: protein structures can be predicted, proteins with certain functions designed, and new measurement technologies such as single-cell and spatial omics are available. But two recent reviews of the field show that most projects in 'AI-first' companies sit in preclinical stages, with several dozen in phase I and phase II and only very few in phase III. The authors' summary is careful: we are at a stage of 'absence of evidence', not 'evidence of absence', for AI translating into clinically relevant impact.

The problem

The value sits in phase II success rates, but AI effort sits in preclinical hit discovery

Searching for your keys under the streetlamp — the light is where the labelled data are, not where the keys fell.

The authors modelled how being faster, cheaper or better at each stage changes the total capitalized cost per successful drug launch. Reducing failure rates at phase II had the greatest impact — the phase where the focus shifts from safety and PK in a small, homogeneous group to efficacy and dosing in diverse patients, raising variability and confounders. Yet current AI effort in small-molecule discovery largely targets preclinical stages such as hit discovery. The authors name this directly as looking for the keys where the light is, rather than where they are likely to be. A worked comparison shows what clinical impact looks like: across ~21,000 compounds from 2000–2015, projects using biomarkers to match drugs to patients cost about half per successful launch compared with those that did not.

What they tried

They audited the data actually fed to these models and found proxies with weak links to what matters

Building a device validated against a bench phantom that only loosely resembles the tissue it will be used on.

Instead of proposing a new algorithm, the authors examine the inputs. Two example datasets show the problem. Liver-organoid cytotoxicity IC50, even corrected for human plasma exposure (Cmax), correlates poorly with clinical drug-induced liver injury liability, with large overlap between risk classes. Thermal shift assays — cheap and high-throughput — are only marginally associated with the harder-to-measure IC50 activity. On the chemical side, the space is roughly 10^60 small molecules, and datasets are local and biased; appending a functional group to one scaffold does something different than on another. Even the benchmark sets are disjoint: across four common ADME datasets, only 0.1% of compounds and 0.6% of scaffolds are shared among all of them.

What they tried

They reframe validation: a model sits inside a project context and feeds a decision, not a test set

Validating a diagnostic by its ROC curve alone, without asking who gets tested, at what threshold, and what happens next.

The authors' central diagram puts model validation in an inner box and surrounds it with two interfaces: the project context that supplies parameters, and the follow-up experiments that act on predictions. Their toxicity example: the concentration cutoff is fixed when the training data are generated, but the dose a compound reaches in humans is only known later, so the model may simply not be relevant. They also warn about information leakage. Publications rarely do prospective validation, and test sets split from the same source dataset inflate performance in a way that does not transfer to future projects. They call this underspecification, and note that benchmark-chasing can create an illusion of progress — Goodhart's law in action.

Your prediction

What happens when two models have nearly the same AUC but are used for different jobs?

Two candidates with identical GPAs — one is right for the lab bench, the other for the sales floor.

The authors construct two hypothetical models with approximately the same area under the curve, the standard summary metric for a binary classifier across all thresholds. One is then used for early selection — pick the top 10% of a screening library to test. The other job is late-stage deselection — throw out the 80% most likely to be toxic and test the rest. If AUC were the right summary, both models should behave about the same in both jobs. Make a call before reading on.

What they found

Equal AUC, opposite winners: one model doubles hit rate in selection, the other is threefold better at screening out toxicity

“If 10% of the top-ranked compounds in the library are selected, the proportion of compounds that are actually in the desired class when tested experimentally would be 40% in case of model 2 compared with 20% in case of model 1.”

p. 12 · Cited · the paper's own words

Selecting the top 10% of the library, model 2 yields 40% true actives among tested compounds versus 20% for model 1 — a twofold advantage. Reverse the job: deselect the 80% ranked most likely toxic and test the remaining 20%, and model 1 leaves only 3.3% undesirable compounds versus 10% for model 2 — roughly threefold better. Same AUC, opposite recommendations. The authors' conclusion is that a generic metric makes it impossible to predict fitness-for-purpose, and that metric choice is one reason published performance and real-world performance diverge. This is the paper's general diagnosis in miniature: the number that gets optimised is not the number that governs the decision.

What they found

Explainable AI on biased chemical data can confidently explain an artefact

A recommendation engine that concludes hospital beds cause illness because sick people are always found in them.

A second failure the authors highlight concerns explainability. In a study of several hundred bitter molecules, an information-gain criterion on fingerprints flagged sugar rings as the features associated with bitterness. That is almost certainly a dataset artefact: natural products are frequently glycosylated and frequently bitter, so the correlation is spurious. Here the artefact is obvious; in other chemical applications it need not be. The authors are careful to say this is not a shortcoming of the algorithms, which are powerful on the right data. Real-world chemical datasets carry synthesis, project, confirmation and reporting biases, and are sparse — that is the problem.

What they found

AlphaFold succeeds where a ground truth exists — and that condition rarely holds elsewhere

A calibration standard exists for a force gauge; there is no equivalent reference block for 'patient improved'.

The authors treat protein structure prediction as the instructive exception. AlphaFold rests on about 50 years of Protein Data Bank depositions, where electron density serves as a suitable ground truth for both training and evaluation. Such a scenario is not available for many other desired predictions. They also separate model validation from process validation. AlphaFold2 reached roughly 90% folding accuracy, but predicted structures gave mixed results for virtual screening — often comparable to traditional homology models — which is the next step in the drug discovery process. Generative small-molecule design is likewise established for improving hit rates, but the contribution is mostly better search of chemical space, still ligand design rather than drug design.

What changed

Their prescription: design the data for the decision, validate inside the process, and close the loop with clinical outcomes

Stop optimising the demo and start running customer discovery — build the thing that changes what someone actually does.

The authors' first recommendation is a two-phase data strategy: an exploratory phase establishing which proxy assay actually predicts a clinically relevant endpoint, then a production phase that scales generation, establishes the applicability domain and integrates with PK models so exposure at the site of action is accounted for. Second, they want preclinical models to learn from clinical observations — including negative and suboptimal outcomes — which is hard because clinical data sit behind different access rules and databases inside the same company. They judge a model's value by two things: its impact on clinical success where applied, and the breadth of its applicability domain across projects. And they frame the cultural shift as moving from 'tech push' to 'science pull'.

What changed

The blockers are also incentives: funding promises, publication pressure, and consortia that pooled data retrospectively

A startup that raised on a bold promise and then has to keep telling the story, whatever the data say.

The authors name non-technical forces. Rising cost per approved drug (Eroom's law) pressures companies to change, while 'AI-first' companies launched on extravagant promises face pressure to go all in on them, which can adversely affect decisions. Publishing incentives reward pushing state-of-the-art on toy datasets whose real-world applicability may be near zero. They also diagnose past consortia. Some IMI efforts fell short because data were pooled retrospectively across companies — clearance data mixed across assay formats, species and thresholds — assuming an equivalence that did not exist, and chemical space coverage was taken as given rather than designed. Their closing metric: what matters is clinical approval rates and novelty in modality and mode-of-action space.

You now have

  • Ligand versus drug — Binding a target is necessary but far from sufficient; roughly 10^6 known ligands correspond to about 10^3 marketed drugs.
  • Conditional biological data — Biological readouts depend on dozens to thousands of unrecorded variables, so labels are conditional and often cannot be made unconditional categories.
  • Use-case-dependent model metrics — Selection, deselection and quantification demand different performance metrics; equal AUC can hide opposite real-world rankings.
  • Process validation vs model validation — A model must be evaluated inside its project context and the decisions it feeds, not only on a held-out test set.
  • Predictive validity of proxy data — Cheap proxy assays like liver organoid cytotoxicity or thermal shift may correlate weakly with the clinical endpoint they stand in for.
