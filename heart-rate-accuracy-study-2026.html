Before the paper

Wrist optical heart rate is inferred from light scattering, and rhythmic motion can masquerade as a pulse

It's like listening for a heartbeat with a stethoscope while someone taps the table at almost exactly the same tempo — the instrument can lock onto the wrong rhythm.

A photoplethysmography (PPG) sensor shines light into the skin and watches how much comes back. Blood volume pulses with each heartbeat, so the reflected-light waveform carries a periodic signal the algorithm turns into beats per minute. The trouble is that the same waveform is contaminated by anything that moves tissue or shifts the optical path: running cadence, muscle tension, sensor rocking. When footstrike frequency sits near heart rate, an algorithm can track the steps instead of the heart — "cadence lock." This paper's activity menu was chosen deliberately to provoke exactly these failure modes, rather than to flatter the sensors.

Before the paper

Each participant wore both devices at once against a chest-strap ECG reference, with wrist assignment randomized

Like an A/B test where both variants run on the same customer at the same moment, so seasonality and customer quality can't explain the gap.

Comparing devices across different people or different workouts invites confounding. The fix here is a within-participant paired design: an Apple Watch on one wrist, the comparator on the other, both referenced to a Polar H10 ECG chest strap worn simultaneously. Dominance matters — your non-dominant wrist moves differently — so watch side was randomized to roughly a 50/50 split. Pairing only works if the clocks agree. Every device, including the chest strap, was paired to its own Wi-Fi phone for network time synchronization, and then each device sample was matched to its nearest-neighbor reference timestamp so accuracy could be scored sample by sample.

Before the paper

Accuracy was scored per session with both MAE and RMSE, plus a separate session-average comparison

MAE is your average monthly burn; RMSE is the metric that screams when one month blows up the runway.

For each session, the paper computes Mean Absolute Error and Root Mean Square Error against the reference. Both are standard, but they answer different questions: because RMSE squares errors before averaging, it is more sensitive to a stream that is usually fine but occasionally badly wrong. A third metric is the session-level average heart rate — what a device reports as your workout mean. That matters especially for screenless wearables, where you never see a live number at all; the summary is the whole experience. One convention to hold onto: every reported result is a difference in error, Apple minus comparator, in bpm. Negative means Apple's error was smaller.

Before the paper

A mixed model with per-participant random intercepts handled repeated measures, and bootstrap intervals handled six simultaneous comparisons

Treating one person's five workouts as five independent data points is like counting a single enthusiastic customer's five reorders as five customers in your conversion rate.

Each participant did several activities, so their sessions are correlated: a person with awkward wrist anatomy will tend to produce high error everywhere. A linear mixed model on the paired difference, with activity as a fixed effect and a random intercept per participant, absorbs that person-level offset instead of pretending every session is independent. The overall number reported is an equally weighted mean of activity means — so a single long activity can't dominate. Uncertainty came from a bias-corrected and accelerated bootstrap clustered by participant, reported at 99% rather than 95% because six devices are being compared at once. Sample size was fixed in advance: at least 50 participants per workout activity per comparator, powered at 80% for a paired effect of about Cohen's dz 0.5 in MAE with Bonferroni adjustment.

The world before

Heart rate is not just a displayed number; it is an input to calorie, VO2 max, and alert features

It's the raw sensor reading at the bottom of a product stack — every derived metric inherits its error, the way a mis-calibrated scale poisons every dosing calculation downstream.

Wearable heart rate tracking is consumed twice: once by the user glancing at a number, and once by the software stack that turns that number into calorie burn, VO2 max, cycle predictions, and high/low heart rate alerts. That makes accuracy structural rather than cosmetic. An error in beats per minute propagates into every derived insight and into clinical-adjacent notifications. Apple frames its new Health Sensing System — updated electrical and optical sensors with new algorithms, enabling features like Recovery HRV and a Readiness app — as the reason a fresh accuracy evaluation was warranted.

The problem

Nobody had run a head-to-head wearable heart rate comparison large enough to support statistically powered conclusions across many activities

Like a market claim built on a dozen friendly beta users instead of a pre-registered trial big enough to survive scrutiny.

The question "which wearable measures heart rate best?" is easy to ask and hard to answer credibly. You need a trusted reference, the same people wearing competing devices at the same time, enough activities to cover real use, and enough participants that small differences are not noise. The paper's stated aim is to evaluate the updated optical sensing system against other devices in the category using an ECG chest strap reference, in a large multi-site head-to-head design with over 1,000 participants. The comparator set was chosen for coverage: brands representing roughly 90% of the worldwide smartwatch category, plus two prominent screenless wearables.

What they tried

1,460 participants across five sites did runs, cycling, HIIT, strength training, and a daily-living protocol wearing seven devices

A multi-site clinical trial protocol, borrowed wholesale for consumer electronics benchmarking.

Comparators were Garmin Forerunner 970, Google Pixel Watch 4, Huawei Watch 5, Samsung Galaxy Watch 8, WHOOP 5, and — worn on an index finger per manufacturer guidance — Oura Ring 5, all latest models as of July–August 2026. The ring was skipped for strength training, per Oura's own advice against lifting in it. Workouts ran 15–20 minutes each: outdoor runs at varied speeds, treadmill intervals, outdoor cycling, HIIT, strength training. A separate Daily Living protocol covered rest, desk work, meal prep, and walking at roughly 20 minutes each. Enrollment reached 1,460 across five sites in California, Texas, and Malaysia; 1,254 contributed at least one paired measurement. Of those, 59% came from third-party CRO sites, 20% were Fitzpatrick skin tone V or VI, 35% female, mean age 39 ± 12 years, mean BMI 26 ± 5.

What they tried

The hardest methodological choice was which data stream each device exposes, since manufacturers publish heart rate at different rates and fidelities

Comparing two dashboards where one logs every event and the other emails you a daily digest — you have to decide what counts as the product's real output.

Three criteria governed stream selection: it had to be visible to the user in the product, it had to carry timestamps good enough for paired analysis, and when several options existed, the highest-frequency one was chosen to limit unknown vendor averaging. In practice that often meant piping Bluetooth Low Energy from the manufacturer's heart rate app into a logger on a separate iPhone, because not all vendors write high-fidelity data to HealthKit or allow export. For Apple Watch, an internal logger captured the single value published to the user every 5 seconds, since background HealthKit writes happen only every 30 seconds. Samsung's background export had no per-sample timestamps at all — only interval bounds, min, max, and one undocumented value — so that value was compared against the reference mean over the interval, the alignment that agreed best.

Every activity crossed with every error measure and every comparator device yields 159 head-to-head comparisons. Each one resolves into three possible verdicts at the 99% confidence level: Apple better, comparator better, or indeterminate. Before you look, place a bet. What they found

Apple Watch had lower error in 139 of 159 comparisons; 18 were indeterminate and only 2 favored a rival

“Apple Watch showed better accuracy with the 99% confidence level in 139, conclusions were indeterminate in 18, and the comparison device's accuracy was higher in 2.”

p. 8 · Cited · the paper's own words

At the headline level, Apple Watch showed statistically significantly better accuracy than every other device for both the Overall Workout and Overall Daily Living protocols, on both RMSE and MAE. Drilling into the 159 activity-by-measure comparisons: 139 favored Apple Watch at 99% confidence, 18 were statistically indeterminate, and 2 favored the comparator. Ignoring significance entirely and just reading point estimates, 155 of 159 leaned Apple's way. For the session-average metric — the number screenless devices actually deliver — Apple Watch was better in 11 of 12 overall comparisons; the exception was Huawei Watch 5 in Overall Workout, where Apple was numerically better but not significantly so.

What they found

The only comparator wins were Pixel Watch 4 at rest, by under 0.5 bpm, while cycling versus Pixel Watch was the one indeterminate workout

The two losses are rounding-error losses — like a competitor beating you on page-load time by three milliseconds.

Within workouts, Apple Watch was statistically better on RMSE and MAE for cycling, outdoor running, indoor interval running, HIIT, and strength training against every device, with one exception: cycling versus Pixel Watch 4, which was indeterminate. Within Daily Living, Apple Watch was better for nearly all devices and activities, with eight indeterminate conclusions concentrated in rest, desk work, and walking. Pixel Watch 4 beat Apple Watch on RMSE and MAE for rest only — by less than 0.5 bpm. The session-average metric was noisier: Apple led for most devices in most activities, but eight of those comparisons were indeterminate, which is what you expect when averaging over a session cancels much of the per-sample error.

What changed

The paper concludes Apple Watch is the most accurate of the wearables tested — a strong claim resting on a design the vendor itself specified and reviewed

It's a founder publishing their own benchmark suite: the methodology can be genuinely rigorous and still be authored by the interested party.

The stated conclusion is direct: better heart rate accuracy than every comparator in a large, statistically powered study, and the most accurate heart rate sensing among devices evaluated. What makes it worth reading as methodology, not just marketing, is the pre-specified power analysis, the within-participant pairing, the 99% intervals adjusted for multiplicity, and the disclosure of awkward details — Samsung's untimestamped background stream, the handful of comparisons Apple did not win. The structural caveat is who ran it. The informed consent form and protocol were approved by multiple review boards within Apple, and Apple managed recruitment and execution at the two Cupertino sites plus Austin, while a third-party CRO ran San Diego and Selangor; those CRO sites accounted for 59% of analyzed participants. Independent replication, particularly of the stream-selection choices, is what would turn this from a credible vendor study into a settled result.

You now have

  • PPG motion artifact and cadence lock — Optical heart rate sensors can track rhythmic body motion instead of blood flow, especially when step rate approaches heart rate.
  • Paired head-to-head design against a reference standard — Wearing both devices and an ECG reference simultaneously turns a device comparison into a within-person paired measurement.
  • RMSE versus MAE — MAE measures typical error while RMSE, by squaring first, exposes streams with rare but large errors.
  • Random intercept for repeated measures — A per-participant random intercept keeps one person's many sessions from being counted as independent evidence.
  • User-facing data stream selection — Benchmarking wearables forces a choice about which of each vendor's differently sampled, differently averaged outputs counts as the product.
